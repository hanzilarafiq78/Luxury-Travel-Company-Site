Brand: ELYSIAN

======================================================================
1. THE PURPOSE AND AMBITION OF THIS PROJECT
======================================================================

ELYSIAN is not intended to look or behave like a conventional travel booking website. The purpose is to create a premium digital experience that makes visitors feel as though they are entering a carefully imagined world of extraordinary travel. The website should communicate discovery, exclusivity, atmosphere, confidence and adventure before a visitor has even opened a destination page. It should feel like the digital home of a high-end exploration company whose journeys are thoughtfully curated rather than mass-produced.

The project must combine two qualities that are often treated separately. It must have the visual ambition and emotional impact of an award-calibre creative website, while also behaving like a credible and usable travel platform. The immersive presentation is not a substitute for the product. Visitors must still be able to discover destinations, compare experiences, understand what is included, choose dates and guests, make a booking, pay through a secure checkout flow, and manage their journeys afterwards. The visual experience should make these tasks more memorable, not make them harder.

The central creative idea is called “The Floating World”. The homepage introduces a restrained, beautifully art-directed 3D globe or miniature floating world as its visual anchor, then transitions into cinematic footage of actual destinations. The primary goal is believable realism. Real aerial, drone and cinematic footage should carry the tropical, jungle, desert and alpine chapters wherever suitable licensed footage is available. Do not force every landscape to be recreated as a fully modelled 3D biome. The globe establishes the ELYSIAN identity and connects the chapters conceptually; the real footage delivers the emotional impact and authenticity of genuine places.

The globe is a visual anchor, not a requirement to build an enormous photorealistic miniature planet. It may be a polished 3D globe, a carefully composed floating-world model, or a hybrid of modest 3D geometry, terrain silhouettes, atmospheric lighting and high-quality textures. Keep it visually sophisticated and credible. Do not use cartoonish, low-poly or obviously game-like assets. The real destination footage should be the hero visual for each regional chapter. Build anticipation gradually and keep interface elements restrained.

The final result should feel polished, deliberate and art-directed. Avoid generic gradients, template-like cards, excessive glassmorphism, random floating objects, meaningless animation and decorative effects that do not support the story. Every major visual decision should have a reason. Typography, spacing, camera movement, lighting, transitions, colour, copywriting and interaction should all contribute to the same identity.

2. BRAND IDENTITY AND CREATIVE POSITIONING
======================================================================

The working brand name is ELYSIAN. The primary tagline is “Travel Beyond the Expected.” The brand represents luxury adventure and exploration. It should not feel like a budget booking aggregator, a generic hotel directory or a conventional tour operator. Its personality should be confident, calm, refined, curious and adventurous.

Luxury should be communicated through restraint and quality rather than visual noise. The interface should use generous spacing, considered typography, excellent imagery, clear hierarchy and purposeful transitions. The world itself can be visually rich, but the surrounding interface should remain controlled. A visitor should always know where they are, what they are looking at and what action they can take next.

Create a visual language that feels editorial and cinematic. Large typography may be used when the story calls for it, but it should not become a constant visual trick. Use a distinctive display typeface for major brand statements and a highly readable supporting typeface for navigation, descriptions, forms and booking information. The selected fonts must be loaded efficiently and tested for legibility at desktop and mobile sizes. Avoid using too many typefaces or weights.

The colour system should be inspired by the natural environments represented on the planet. Deep midnight tones and near-black atmospheric backgrounds can establish the opening scene. Tropical chapters can introduce controlled turquoise and warm coastal highlights. The jungle can use deep botanical greens. The desert can introduce sand, amber and muted copper. The alpine region can introduce cool stone, snow and blue-grey tones. These environmental colours should emerge through the 3D world and carefully chosen interface accents. Do not turn the entire website into a collection of unrelated colour themes. The brand must remain recognisable from beginning to end.

The ELYSIAN wordmark should be treated as a carefully placed editorial element. During the opening, it should appear with confidence and sufficient breathing room. It should not compete with the planet. Navigation should remain minimal and legible, with clear routes to Explore, Destinations, Experiences and the user's account or booking area. A persistent header may adapt subtly as the story progresses, but it must not jump, flicker or obstruct important content.

The writing style should be evocative without becoming vague. Headlines can create emotion, but supporting copy must explain what a destination or experience actually offers. “Find Your Own Paradise” can introduce the tropical region, while the accompanying description should explain the coast, accommodation style, activities and type of traveller the experience suits. The platform must balance aspiration with useful information.

3. THE HOMEPAGE AS ONE CONTINUOUS JOURNEY
======================================================================

The homepage must be designed as one connected cinematic narrative with eight major chapters. The experience is hybrid rather than exclusively 3D: a persistent 3D globe/visual anchor may be presented through one Three.js scene, while full-screen or large-format real-world destination videos provide the main regional imagery. The visitor should feel guided through one coherent ELYSIAN journey, not presented with unrelated stock-video sections. Use the globe, chapter typography, location markers, colour grading, sound design where appropriate, and carefully designed transitions to connect the real places. Do not require the camera to physically fly through fully modelled 3D versions of every location.

The scroll interaction is the main storytelling mechanism. Scrolling down should move the visitor forward through the narrative. Scrolling up should reverse the journey naturally. The camera must not suddenly teleport, reset to an unrelated position or switch to an entirely different scene. Every chapter should have a clear entrance, a readable visual moment and a deliberate transition into the next chapter. The camera path must be planned in three-dimensional space before detailed animation is added. It must never pass through terrain, buildings, large trees or other solid objects. If a camera move is not physically plausible or visually comfortable, redesign the route rather than hiding the problem with a hard cut.

The opening chapter is called “The Invitation”. The visitor initially enters a dark, atmospheric environment with a restrained sense of depth. The first moments should create curiosity. A subtle field of distant stars, soft atmospheric haze or faint environmental glow may establish scale, but these effects must remain tasteful and lightweight. The planet should not immediately fill the entire screen. It should emerge gradually from darkness, allowing its silhouette and lighting to create anticipation. The ELYSIAN wordmark and tagline should appear as part of the composition, with the main message “Travel Beyond the Expected.” The visitor should understand that this is an invitation into a different kind of travel experience.

The opening camera movement should be slow, stable and intentional. It may begin at a distant position and approach the planet while revealing its shape. The planet should not spin uncontrollably. Any rotation must contribute to the composition and must not conflict with the scroll-controlled camera movement. The camera should settle into a position that gives the visitor a clear view of the world before the next chapter begins.

The second chapter is called “The World Revealed”. Reveal the globe and use subtle orbit, lighting, markers or animated geographical highlights to introduce the range of journeys. The globe should suggest a connected collection of extraordinary places without pretending that unrelated real destinations are literally adjacent on one physical miniature planet. It is acceptable for the globe to be symbolic and editorial. Its purpose is to establish the journey structure and transition into authentic destination footage.

During this chapter, the interface can introduce a short statement about the ELYSIAN approach to travel. Keep the copy concise enough that the visitor can continue observing the world. A small location or chapter indicator may help orient the visitor, but it should not resemble a technical dashboard. The experience should feel like guided discovery rather than a game interface.

The third chapter is called “The Tropical Frontier”. The camera travels towards the tropical coastline and brings the visitor close enough to appreciate its detail. The environment should include clear turquoise water, a natural shoreline, coastal cliffs or rock formations, tropical vegetation, palms and a secluded architectural feature such as a private villa or retreat. A waterfall or stream may connect the inland terrain to the sea. The coastline should feel designed as a real place with readable geography, not as a random collection of tropical props.

Lighting in this chapter should feel warm, bright and inviting. The water should have a convincing visual response without requiring an expensive simulation. Use an efficient material and restrained shader effects. The shoreline should have a believable transition between water, sand, rock and vegetation. The villa or retreat should be integrated into the landscape and should not look like an object floating above the terrain.

As the camera reaches the tropical region, the headline “Find Your Own Paradise” should appear in HTML interface content. Supporting text should describe the feeling and substance of the destination, such as secluded coastal stays, private experiences, island exploration or water-based activities, depending on the actual product content. The text must remain readable over the scene. Use a controlled text panel, gradient backing or carefully chosen placement if necessary, but do not cover the most important part of the 3D environment.

The fourth chapter is called “Into the Wild”. The camera should leave the coast through a natural geographical route and enter the jungle or forest region. The transition should be visible and gradual. The visitor may pass over a river valley, follow a winding watercourse or travel across a green foothill before arriving at denser forest. The camera should not suddenly jump from the beach to a jungle clearing.

The jungle environment should feel layered and alive. Use variations in tree height, canopy density, ground vegetation, rocks, rivers, waterfalls and winding trails. A remote lodge or architectural retreat may serve as a focal point. The environment should have enough variation to avoid looking like a flat green carpet. Create depth by combining foreground, middle-distance and background elements. The scene should remain visually understandable from the camera's intended path.

Lighting should become more filtered and atmospheric. Canopy shadows, soft shafts of light and reflections near water may add depth, but do not use heavy volumetric effects everywhere. The jungle should feel rich and mysterious without becoming so dark that its details disappear. The headline “Lose Yourself in the Extraordinary” should introduce this chapter. Supporting copy should explain the type of exploration available, such as guided wilderness journeys, remote lodges, nature experiences or river excursions. These details must correspond to actual experience data when the platform is connected to real inventory.

The fifth chapter is called “The Silent Desert”. The camera should travel out of the jungle through a believable transition zone. Vegetation can become less dense, the ground can become rockier and the landscape can gradually open into dry valleys and dunes. This transition is essential because it makes the planet feel geographically connected. Do not hide a discontinuity with a flash, fade or sudden camera cut.

The desert should have a strong and elegant silhouette. Use rolling dunes, rocky formations, dry valleys and a remote retreat or architectural structure that feels integrated into the environment. The terrain should show variation in height and direction. Avoid repeating identical dune shapes or using a single noisy displacement texture that makes the landscape look artificial. The material should respond to warm light in a controlled way. Long shadows can help communicate the shape of the terrain.

The headline for this chapter is “Discover the Beauty of Solitude.” Supporting content should communicate quiet, space, remote luxury and carefully curated desert experiences. The environment should feel peaceful rather than hostile. If a retreat is shown, it should be positioned with a reason, such as a view over a valley or a sheltered location near a rock formation.

The sixth chapter is called “Above the Clouds”. The camera continues towards the alpine region, moving from higher dry foothills into mountain terrain. The transition should show the land rising. The visitor should see the first slopes, then the larger mountain forms, before arriving at a dramatic viewpoint. The mountains should have a readable hierarchy. There should be foreground slopes, middle-distance peaks and a distant atmospheric range. Snow should be placed where it makes geographical and visual sense rather than covering every surface uniformly.

The alpine region should include snow-covered peaks, exposed rock, valleys, alpine forest and perhaps a remote cabin or mountain retreat. The scene should feel expansive and quiet. Lighting should be cooler than in the desert, while preserving enough warmth and contrast to avoid a flat blue image. Atmospheric perspective can help communicate scale. The headline “Find Your Next Summit” should introduce the chapter, with supporting text about alpine escapes, guided mountain journeys, secluded cabins or other relevant experiences.

The seventh chapter is called “The Expedition Collection”. At this point, the visual storytelling begins to connect directly to the travel product. The planet should remain present, perhaps as a carefully framed background or central visual anchor, while the interface gives more space to real destination and experience information. This is not the moment to abandon the 3D world abruptly. The camera can pull back slightly or settle into a composed orbit so that the content layer can become more prominent.

The expedition collection should feature actual content records from the platform. Each experience card should explain what the visitor is considering. It should include a destination or experience name, location, duration, category, starting price where available, a short description and a clear action to view details. The card design should feel like a premium editorial travel publication rather than a generic ecommerce grid. Use strong photography where available, consistent image treatment, concise information and clear interaction states. Do not invent availability, ratings, reviews, suppliers or prices. If the project begins with sample content, label it clearly as demonstration content and keep it separate from real bookable inventory.

The eighth chapter is called “Begin Your Journey”. The camera gradually pulls back to reveal the entire floating planet again. The visitor should now understand the relationship between the regions and the experiences presented earlier. The final composition should feel like a satisfying conclusion to the journey, not merely another section. The final headline is “Your Next Adventure Begins Here.” Include a clear call to action that takes the visitor to Explore or the booking discovery flow. The final screen should leave the visitor with a strong sense of place and a clear next step.

4. GEOGRAPHY, WORLD DESIGN AND SCENE CONTINUITY
======================================================================

The floating planet must be planned as a single geographical object before individual props are modelled. Begin with the overall silhouette, terrain elevations, coastline, water boundary and biome placement. Decide where each region sits in relation to the others and how the camera will travel between them. This planning stage is more important than adding decorative detail early. A beautiful set of assets will not create a convincing world if the terrain layout is incoherent.

Use one shared coordinate system for the planet, terrain, architecture, vegetation and landmarks. Every asset should have a meaningful position, scale and orientation in that coordinate system. The tropical, jungle, desert and alpine areas should connect through actual terrain geometry or carefully designed transition meshes. Avoid visible gaps, intersecting surfaces, abrupt texture boundaries and floating props. A visitor should be able to understand how the landscape continues beyond the current camera framing.

The world can be stylised rather than physically exact, but it must be internally consistent. The planet may have an intentionally designed miniature-world appearance, yet the terrain should still respond to gravity, elevation and geography. Rivers should follow plausible downhill routes. Waterfalls should connect to water surfaces. Buildings should sit on stable ground. Trees should grow from terrain rather than hover above it. Mountain ranges should emerge from foothills. Dunes should flow with the larger terrain forms.

Create a camera route map before final animation. The route should identify the starting position, major chapter viewpoints, transition corridors and final pullback. Each viewpoint should be tested for composition. The camera must have enough clearance from terrain and assets. It should not clip through tree canopies, buildings, cliffs or the planet surface. If a chapter needs a closer view, shape the terrain and place assets to support that view rather than forcing the camera through an unsuitable space.

The planet's scale should support both wide establishing views and close-up regional views. A planet that looks attractive from far away may not contain enough usable detail when the camera approaches. An excessively large world may be difficult to model, load and render on modest hardware. Choose a practical scale and use a deliberate level-of-detail strategy. The visitor should never notice that distant details are simplified.

The atmosphere surrounding the planet should create separation from the background without making the object look as if it has an unrelated glowing outline. Use a restrained atmospheric shell, carefully tuned rim lighting or a subtle shader effect. The atmosphere should not obscure terrain detail. Any particles or stars should be sparse, purposeful and inexpensive to render.

5. BLENDER PRODUCTION WORKFLOW
======================================================================

Blender is the primary environment for building and preparing the 3D world and its assets. Develop the world in manageable stages. Do not attempt to model the entire planet at maximum detail in one operation. Begin with a blockout that establishes the planet silhouette, major terrain elevations, biome locations, camera route and landmark positions. Review this blockout in the browser or in a lightweight preview before investing time in detailed modelling.

The Blender project must remain organised. Use clear collection names for the main terrain, ocean, atmosphere, tropical environment, jungle environment, desert environment, alpine environment, architecture, vegetation, rocks and additional landmark groups. Object names should describe their purpose. Avoid names such as Cube.001 or Object.043 in the final exported scene. Meaningful names make it possible to target individual components in Three.js and make future revisions safer.

Keep an editable Blender source file for the master world. Export web-ready GLB files for the browser. The export strategy should be chosen according to how the world will be loaded. If one combined GLB is too large or prevents progressive loading, separate the world into logical region assets while preserving the same coordinate system and object transforms. The camera should be able to move continuously across these assets without visible loading seams. If assets are loaded separately, preload the next region before the camera reaches it.

Use PBR materials with restrained, consistent roughness, metallic and normal-map settings. Avoid excessive texture resolution. A material should be judged in the actual Three.js lighting environment, not only inside Blender. Colour management and lighting differences can make an asset look very different in the browser. Test representative exports early so that the team does not build the entire world around a material setup that later needs to be replaced.

The terrain should be detailed enough to look convincing at the planned camera distance, but avoid unnecessary polygon density. Use geometry where the silhouette or close-up form requires it, and use normal maps, roughness variation or carefully authored material detail for smaller surface features. Do not add dense geometry simply because it looks impressive in the Blender viewport. The target is a responsive web experience.

Vegetation requires special attention because repeated leaves, branches and grass can quickly increase draw calls and memory usage. Reuse geometry where possible, use instancing in Three.js for repeated elements and keep the number of unique materials under control. Distant vegetation can use simpler representations. The close-up camera path should determine where the highest detail is necessary.

Architectural assets should be designed as focal points, not random decoration. The tropical villa, jungle lodge, desert retreat and alpine cabin should each have a clear silhouette and a believable relationship with the terrain. Their materials and proportions should belong to the same design world. Do not use unrelated asset packs that make the planet feel like a collage of different visual styles. If third-party assets are used, review their licences and record the source and licence in the project documentation.

Prepare exported GLB files for browser use. Apply transforms where appropriate, remove hidden or unused objects, remove unnecessary cameras and lights from exports if they are not required, and check that material assignments survive export. Use Draco geometry compression and KTX2 texture compression where supported by the chosen loading pipeline and where they improve the actual result. Compression must be tested for visual quality and compatibility. Do not assume that enabling every compression option automatically improves performance.

Maintain a simple asset report that records each exported file, its approximate size, its contents, its intended loading stage and any known limitations. Keep the original .blend files. Never overwrite a working source file without preserving a recoverable version. The project should be easy to continue if a model needs to be revised later.

Build progressively and test early, especially on modest hardware. Use simplified preview assets during early interface work. The final scene should be tested in the target browser and on a lower-powered device profile. A visually impressive scene that cannot load reliably is not a successful result.

6. THREE.JS RENDERING AND APPLICATION ARCHITECTURE
======================================================================

The 3D experience should use one coherent rendering architecture. Choose either direct Three.js or React Three Fiber based on the existing project structure and the team's actual familiarity. Do not mix both approaches without a clear technical reason. If the repository already has a working Three.js setup, inspect it and extend it rather than replacing the architecture unnecessarily.

The homepage should use a persistent renderer, scene and primary camera. The canvas should remain mounted while the visitor scrolls through the eight chapters. Do not create a new renderer for every section. Recreating the renderer or scene repeatedly can cause memory leaks, visual flicker, loading delays and inconsistent camera state. Integrate the 3D canvas with the page layout so that it can remain fixed or sticky while HTML chapter content moves through the viewport.

Separate scene setup, asset loading, camera control, lighting, animation coordination and user-interface content. The implementation should be understandable and maintainable. The camera path should be represented by a dedicated controller or a well-defined set of keyframes. Do not scatter camera position changes across unrelated React components or section effects.

The camera controller should interpolate between carefully authored points and orientations. Use smooth interpolation that preserves the intended path and avoids sudden acceleration. The camera should look at meaningful targets rather than relying on arbitrary fixed rotations. Consider using a spline or a carefully constructed sequence of camera keyframes. Test the camera path in both directions. Reverse scrolling must return the camera to the exact corresponding state without drift.

The scene should load only the assets required for the experience and progressively prepare assets before they are needed. If the next region is not yet loaded, avoid showing a blank area or allowing the camera to reach missing content. Use a loading strategy that accounts for network speed and device capability. Provide a graceful loading state that matches the brand rather than displaying a generic browser spinner.

Use a controlled lighting setup. Lighting should make each biome feel distinct while preserving the overall visual identity. Avoid excessive real-time shadow casting from every object. Bake or simplify lighting where practical, and use selective shadows for important objects. Post-processing should be restrained and evaluated against performance. Bloom, depth of field, film grain or chromatic effects should only be used if they genuinely improve the image. Never stack effects merely to make the scene look more cinematic.

The renderer should adapt to device capability. Use a sensible pixel ratio cap rather than rendering at an unnecessarily high resolution on every display. Reduce expensive effects and shadow quality on mobile or lower-powered devices. Use instancing for repeated vegetation and props. Consider level of detail for distant geometry. Dispose of geometries, materials, textures and event listeners when assets or components are genuinely removed. Ensure that the renderer is cleaned up correctly when the page is left.

The scene must have a reliable fallback. If WebGL is unavailable, initialization fails or the device cannot support the experience, the visitor must still be able to use the homepage. Provide a high-quality static or lightly animated visual alternative and preserve all chapter text, navigation and calls to action as normal HTML. The website must not become an empty page because the 3D canvas failed.

Do not use React state updates for every animation frame. High-frequency rendering and camera movement should be handled by the rendering loop and animation system. React should manage meaningful application state, not every frame of the 3D scene. Keep the boundary between the 3D runtime and the normal application interface clear.

7. GSAP, SCROLLTRIGGER AND CAMERA STORYTELLING
======================================================================

GSAP and ScrollTrigger should coordinate the scroll-driven narrative. The homepage should have one master animation timeline or one clearly managed orchestration system that controls the camera and chapter transitions. Avoid several independent timelines competing to set the same camera properties. Conflicting timelines are a common cause of jitter, unexpected jumps and broken reverse scrolling.

Create named timeline labels corresponding to the eight chapters: invitation, worldReveal, tropicalFrontier, intoTheWild, silentDesert, aboveTheClouds, expeditionCollection and finalDeparture. These labels should make the animation structure understandable to another developer. The scroll position should map predictably to the progress of the master timeline. The visitor should be able to pause at any chapter by stopping scroll, and the scene should hold a deliberate composition rather than continuing to animate without purpose.

Use scrub behaviour carefully. The camera should respond smoothly to scrolling without feeling disconnected from the visitor's input. Avoid excessive smoothing that makes the camera lag far behind the scroll position. The animation should feel controlled and premium. Section transitions should have enough duration to communicate movement, but they should not make the visitor feel trapped in a long animation before reaching useful content.

All transitions must be reversible. When the visitor scrolls upwards, the camera, chapter text, opacity, scale and other animated properties should return to their corresponding earlier states. Avoid one-way callbacks that permanently change scene state. If an asset visibility toggle or loading event is required, make sure it does not create a visual mismatch when the visitor reverses direction.

The chapter text should animate in a restrained way. It may fade, move a small distance or reveal through a mask, but it should not use excessive bouncing, spinning or elastic movement. Text transitions must preserve legibility. Avoid animating every word independently unless there is a strong art-direction reason. The camera should remain the main motion element.

Use ScrollTrigger pinning only where it supports the experience. The pinned 3D canvas can remain fixed while chapter content advances. The page must still have a logical document structure and accessible content. Pinning should be tested on mobile browsers because viewport resizing, browser chrome and touch scrolling can behave differently from desktop. Recalculate or refresh ScrollTrigger when layout changes, and ensure that refreshes do not reset the camera to an incorrect state.

Support prefers-reduced-motion. Visitors who request reduced motion should receive a simplified experience with minimal camera movement or a static sequence of destination imagery. All content and navigation must remain available. Reduced-motion handling should be designed into the animation architecture rather than added as a last-minute patch.

The experience must work with a mouse wheel, trackpad, keyboard navigation and touch scrolling. Do not hijack normal page scrolling in a way that makes the website difficult to use. If a custom scroll system is considered, it must be justified, accessible and tested thoroughly. Native scrolling with ScrollTrigger is the preferred starting point.

8. INTERFACE DESIGN AND CONTENT PRESENTATION
======================================================================

The interface must complement the 3D world rather than compete with it. Build the page as a premium editorial travel experience. The layout should use strong visual hierarchy, careful spacing, concise copy and consistent alignment. Navigation should be simple and predictable. The visitor should never have to guess how to reach destinations, experiences, booking information or their account.

The desktop homepage may use a persistent canvas occupying the visual stage, with chapter copy positioned in carefully composed areas around it. Text should not obscure important 3D details. Use responsive layout rules so that the text and planet remain balanced at different screen widths. On smaller screens, the design may simplify the 3D composition and use more conventional section layouts if that produces a clearer experience.

The website should not depend on the 3D canvas to communicate essential information. All headings, descriptions, navigation items, buttons, prices, forms and booking details must exist as accessible HTML. Text rendered only inside WebGL is not acceptable for essential content. Ensure that buttons have clear labels, keyboard focus states and appropriate semantic elements. Images should have meaningful alternative text when they convey content.

Use consistent interaction states. Navigation links, buttons, cards, filters and form controls should have clear hover, focus, active, disabled and loading states. Hover effects should be subtle and should not be the only way to reveal important information. Touch users must have access to the same essential actions.

Destination and experience cards should use consistent image ratios and carefully edited content. Avoid overly dense cards. A premium card should allow the visitor to understand the experience quickly and decide whether to open it. The card should link to a detailed page with more complete information, not attempt to display every detail at once.

The booking interface should prioritise clarity over visual decoration. Dates, guest counts, availability, included services, exclusions, cancellation terms, taxes, fees and total price must be visible before the customer confirms payment. Do not hide important costs behind vague labels. If a price is indicative, label it as indicative. If availability has not been checked against a supplier or booking system, do not present it as confirmed.

9. COMPLETE WEBSITE STRUCTURE
======================================================================

The homepage is the immersive introduction, but ELYSIAN must be a complete multi-page platform. Build a consistent design system that carries the brand into functional pages without forcing every page to use the same heavy 3D experience. The homepage is the flagship visual moment. Other pages should prioritise clarity, speed, useful information and conversion.

The Explore page should help visitors discover available destinations and experiences. It should provide search, filters and sorting that work with actual platform data. Filters may include destination, experience type, duration, travel season, budget range and other relevant attributes. The interface should make selected filters visible and easy to clear. Empty search results should explain that no matching experiences were found and provide a useful way to broaden the search.

The Destinations page should present the geographical places available through ELYSIAN. Each destination should have a clear image, short introduction and link to a dedicated destination detail page. The destination detail page should explain the location, best time to visit where known, highlights, travel considerations, available experiences and relevant practical information. Do not invent factual travel advice. Content that depends on current conditions should be reviewed and maintained.

The Experiences page should present bookable or enquiry-based journeys. Experience detail pages should include a strong introduction, gallery, itinerary, duration, location, group size where applicable, accommodation information, inclusions, exclusions, requirements, cancellation terms and pricing information. The booking call to action should lead into the correct booking flow for that experience. If an experience requires a concierge enquiry rather than instant booking, explain that clearly.

The Booking page should guide the customer through experience selection, dates, guest details and any required options. It should show the price calculation transparently. The Checkout page should collect the information required to complete the booking and process payment securely. The user should receive a clear confirmation only after the server has verified the successful payment or the relevant booking state.

The Login and Register pages should support customer accounts. The customer dashboard should show upcoming journeys, past bookings, booking status, profile details and relevant account actions. The My Journeys area should make it easy for customers to review their reservations and retrieve important trip information. Protect all customer-specific records so that one customer cannot view or change another customer's information.

The About page should explain the ELYSIAN philosophy, approach to curation and standards. The Concierge page should provide a clear enquiry route for bespoke trips or experiences that cannot be booked immediately. The Contact page should provide genuine contact details only when they have been supplied and verified. Do not invent a business address, phone number, email address or social account.

The legal and policy pages should include Terms and Conditions, Privacy Policy and Cancellation Policy. These must be treated as real product requirements, not placeholder links. Their content should be reviewed for the operating jurisdiction and business model before launch. Do not generate legal claims or promise protections that have not been confirmed by the business.

The Admin area must be protected by server-side authentication and role-based authorization. It should allow authorised staff to manage destinations, experiences, images, itinerary content, pricing, availability rules, bookings, customer enquiries and relevant site content. Administrative functions must not be exposed merely by hiding links in the frontend.

10. BOOKING, INVENTORY AND BUSINESS LOGIC
======================================================================

The booking system must distinguish between content that is displayed for inspiration and inventory that can genuinely be reserved. A destination page may exist without having live availability. An experience may be enquiry-only. A sample experience may be used during development. These states must be represented honestly in the application and clearly in the interface.

The booking flow should begin with a specific experience or a defined search result. The customer selects dates and guest numbers. The server then validates the selection against the experience's booking rules and availability source. The server calculates the applicable price, including any options, taxes or fees that the business has defined. The frontend must not be trusted to provide the final price or determine that a booking is available.

Availability and pricing must be recalculated or revalidated on the server before a booking is confirmed. The application should handle the possibility that availability changes between the initial search and checkout. If the selected dates are no longer available, explain the problem and allow the customer to choose another option. Do not silently substitute dates, prices or experiences.

A booking should have a clear lifecycle. It may begin as a pending booking while payment is being processed, become confirmed after verified payment and business rules are satisfied, or become failed, cancelled or refunded where applicable. The exact states should be designed around the actual business process. Each state change should be recorded so that staff can understand what happened and when.

If the platform does not yet connect to real travel suppliers, create a clearly labelled demonstration inventory for development. Do not claim that demonstration data represents live supplier availability. Do not fabricate supplier connections, booking references or successful external reservations. The architecture should allow a future supplier integration, but the application must accurately describe what is and is not connected today.

The concierge enquiry process should be separate from instant booking. A customer should be able to submit a request with relevant travel preferences and contact details. The server should validate and store the enquiry, and the admin area should allow authorised staff to review and update its status. Provide a clear confirmation that the enquiry was received, but do not imply that a trip is reserved until the business confirms it.

11. BACKEND, DATABASE AND APPLICATION STRUCTURE
======================================================================

Before changing the repository, inspect the existing project thoroughly. Identify its framework, package manager, directory structure, existing routes, components, database setup, authentication, environment configuration, styling system and current implementation status. Do not delete or replace working code simply because a different structure is preferred. Preserve existing decisions where they are sound. Prepare a concise audit of what already exists, what is incomplete and what needs to be built before making major changes.

Use the existing architecture if it is suitable. If the project is new, a practical stack may include Next.js, React, TypeScript, Three.js or React Three Fiber, GSAP with ScrollTrigger, Tailwind CSS or the project's existing styling approach, a relational database such as PostgreSQL, and a suitable ORM such as Prisma or Drizzle. These are suggested choices, not instructions to force dependencies into a repository that already has a good equivalent.

The backend should own all sensitive business logic. It should validate user input, calculate authoritative prices, check permissions, create bookings, communicate with payment services and process webhooks. The browser should never be trusted to decide that a payment succeeded or that a booking is confirmed.

The database should represent users, roles, destinations, experiences, experience images, itineraries, pricing rules, availability information, bookings, payment records and concierge enquiries as appropriate to the actual product. Use clear relationships and constraints. Store monetary values in a reliable numeric representation, such as integer minor currency units, and record the currency explicitly. Avoid floating-point arithmetic for financial calculations.

Design database access so that customers can only access their own records unless they have an explicitly authorised administrative role. Validate all incoming identifiers and parameters. Use pagination for large lists. Add appropriate indexes for frequently queried fields such as slugs, destination relationships, booking status and user identifiers. Use migrations and preserve data when changing the schema.

Environment variables should be documented in an example environment file without real secrets. Never commit API keys, database passwords, webhook secrets or private credentials. The client bundle must not contain server-only secrets. Keep development, test and production configuration separate.

12. PAYMENTS AND CHECKOUT SECURITY
======================================================================

If Stripe is selected, use its supported server-side payment flow and test mode during development. The server should create the payment object using an authoritative amount derived from the validated booking. The frontend may collect payment details using Stripe's approved client components, but it must never decide the final amount or mark the booking as paid by itself.

Verify webhook signatures on the server. Webhook processing must be idempotent so that repeated delivery of the same event does not create duplicate bookings, duplicate payment records or repeated business actions. Record the relevant provider event identifiers and payment references. Handle successful payments, failed payments, cancellations and refunds according to the selected payment flow.

A browser redirect to a success page is not proof of payment. The application should display a confirmed state only after the server has verified the payment result through the appropriate provider response or verified webhook process. If payment is still processing, show a pending state and explain what the customer should expect next.

Never store raw card numbers or card security codes in the application's database. Do not expose secret keys to the browser. Use secure transport in production, validate webhook endpoints and ensure that payment errors do not leak sensitive internal information. Provide clear customer-facing error messages without exposing stack traces or provider secrets.

During development, use test credentials and clearly label test transactions. Do not claim that real payments are being accepted until the production configuration, business account and operational process have been verified.

13. AUTHENTICATION, PRIVACY AND SECURITY
======================================================================

Authentication must use a reputable, secure implementation. Passwords must be hashed using a modern password-hashing algorithm through a trusted library or authentication provider. Never store plaintext passwords. Password reset flows must use secure, time-limited tokens and must not reveal whether an arbitrary email address has an account.

Protect private routes on the server. Hiding a dashboard link or admin menu item in the frontend is not authorization. Every sensitive operation must verify the current user's identity and permissions on the server. Administrative roles should be assigned through a controlled process, not through a value that any user can submit from the browser.

Validate and sanitise user input on the server. Use safe database query patterns and protect against injection, cross-site scripting, cross-site request forgery where relevant, insecure direct object references and broken access control. Apply rate limits to sensitive actions such as login, password reset, enquiry submission and payment-related endpoints. Use secure cookie settings and appropriate session expiry.

Collect only the personal information required to operate the service. Explain why information is collected and how it is used. Do not expose customer information in public API responses, logs or analytics. Avoid logging passwords, payment details, session tokens or sensitive personal information. Provide a way for authorised staff to handle customer data requests in accordance with the final privacy policy and applicable law.

14. PERFORMANCE AND RESPONSIVE EXPERIENCE
======================================================================

Performance is a core design requirement, not a final optimisation task. The 3D homepage will be demanding, so it must be designed around a realistic asset and rendering budget. Measure actual loading and runtime behaviour instead of claiming performance based on appearance. A target of smooth desktop rendering is useful, but report measured frame rates and device conditions rather than promising a fixed result without testing.

Use compressed geometry and textures where appropriate. Keep material counts controlled. Reuse repeated objects through instancing. Simplify distant assets and avoid rendering hidden objects unnecessarily. Limit dynamic shadows and expensive post-processing. Load the highest-detail assets only when they are needed. Preload the next region before the camera arrives, but avoid downloading the entire world at once on a slow connection if a staged approach is more appropriate.

The initial page load should prioritise the content and assets needed for the opening view. Do not block the entire website while every destination asset downloads. The visitor should see a branded loading state and receive meaningful progress feedback where possible. If the 3D experience cannot load, the normal page content must remain available.

Mobile should be treated as a designed experience rather than a compressed desktop layout. The camera framing, text placement, navigation and interaction should be reconsidered for narrow screens. Mobile devices may use a reduced-detail planet, fewer vegetation instances, simpler shadows and less post-processing. If a continuous close-up camera journey is too expensive on a particular device, provide a deliberate simplified sequence rather than allowing performance to collapse.

Forms, filters and booking steps should remain easy to use on touchscreens. Inputs should have suitable sizes, labels and validation messages. Avoid hover-only interactions. Ensure that important content does not overflow horizontally and that sticky or pinned elements do not trap the user.

15. ACCESSIBILITY AND INCLUSIVE INTERACTION
======================================================================

The website must remain usable without the 3D experience. Provide semantic HTML, logical heading structure, accessible navigation, visible keyboard focus, descriptive button labels and meaningful alternative text. Ensure that interactive controls can be operated by keyboard and touch. Do not use colour alone to communicate booking status, validation errors or availability.

Respect the visitor's prefers-reduced-motion setting. A reduced-motion version should preserve the same content and destinations while reducing camera travel, parallax and nonessential transitions. Avoid flashing effects and rapid movement. Make sure text remains readable over imagery and that contrast is sufficient.

The 3D canvas should not prevent screen readers from reaching the actual page content. Essential information must be represented in the document. If the canvas is decorative for a particular section, mark it appropriately so that assistive technology does not announce meaningless graphics. Loading states and errors should be communicated in accessible text.

16. CONTENT MANAGEMENT AND ADMINISTRATION
======================================================================

The admin experience should allow authorised staff to maintain the content that visitors see. Staff should be able to create and edit destinations, experiences, descriptions, images, itineraries, pricing information and enquiry records. If availability is managed manually, the interface should make that limitation clear. If a supplier integration is added later, its data should be distinguished from manually maintained content.

Content forms should validate required fields and provide helpful errors. Slugs should be unique. Images should be validated for file type and size, and uploaded files should be stored through a suitable storage solution rather than embedded directly in database records. Provide image alternative text and useful metadata fields where appropriate.

Administrative changes should be reflected on the public website through a reliable data-fetching strategy. Avoid hardcoding the same experience content in several unrelated components. Use reusable content models and shared presentation components. Record important administrative actions where an audit trail is needed for business operations.

21. UPDATED VISUAL DIRECTION: PHOTOREALISTIC HYBRID EXPERIENCE
======================================================================

This section is the current approved creative direction and takes precedence over any earlier wording in this blueprint that implies every biome must be a fully modelled 3D environment or that the camera must physically travel through one continuous 3D terrain mesh. The goal is a premium, realistic travel experience. Visual authenticity is more important than using 3D for its own sake.

The recommended approach is a hybrid production: use a restrained, high-quality 3D globe or floating-world visual as the recurring ELYSIAN identity, and use cinematic footage of real places as the primary imagery for the destination chapters. The globe can appear in the opening, the world-reveal chapter, as a transition device between destinations, during the expedition collection and in the final pullback. It does not need to contain a fully detailed, geographically continuous miniature version of every destination. It can be symbolic, art-directed and visually coherent.

The regional chapters should prioritise real footage. The Tropical Frontier should use genuine aerial/coastal footage of clear water, islands, beaches, cliffs, palms and resorts. Into the Wild should use genuine rainforest, jungle canopy, rivers, waterfalls and remote lodges. The Silent Desert should use real dunes, rock formations, desert valleys, changing light and desert retreats. Above the Clouds should use real alpine peaks, snow, mountain valleys, forests, cabins and cloud movement. Prefer footage that has a strong cinematic composition, stable movement, natural light and sufficient resolution for large desktop displays.

Do not use random stock clips merely because they match a keyword. Curate each chapter as a coherent sequence. Footage should have compatible colour, exposure, contrast, camera movement and aspect ratio. Avoid obvious mismatches in time of day, weather or visual quality within one chapter. Where possible, use a small number of excellent clips rather than a rapid montage. Use slow, deliberate edits and let the destination breathe. The footage should feel like a luxury travel film, not a generic stock-video slideshow.

Transitions should make the shift between the 3D globe and real footage feel intentional. Possible treatments include a destination marker illuminating on the globe, a controlled push-in towards a selected region, a brief atmospheric dissolve, a match cut from globe texture/shape to a real aerial shot, or a clean editorial transition with typography. Do not pretend the real-world clips are a continuous physical camera flight if they are not. Avoid abrupt hard cuts unless deliberately used as an editorial choice. Every transition must work when scrolling both down and up.

The eight chapters remain: The Invitation, The World Revealed, The Tropical Frontier, Into the Wild, The Silent Desert, Above the Clouds, The Expedition Collection and Begin Your Journey. Their purpose and headline copy remain intact. Their visual implementation changes as follows. The Invitation introduces the brand and globe. The World Revealed presents the globe with subtle rotation, highlights or markers. Each of the four regional chapters is led by real footage with restrained overlays and concise copy. The Expedition Collection combines authentic destination stills or footage thumbnails with real platform content. Begin Your Journey returns to the globe and closes the story with the main call to action.

FOOTAGE SOURCING AND LICENSING

Potential discovery sources include Pexels, Mixkit and other reputable stock libraries, alongside paid libraries if the project budget allows. These are discovery sources, not blanket permission to use every clip. Check the licence for every individual asset at the time of download. Confirm that commercial website use, editing, cropping, looping, web distribution and any required attribution are permitted. Check restrictions on redistribution, standalone resale, identifiable people, trademarks, private property, drone restrictions and editorial-only footage. Save the original asset page, creator name, download date, licence text or screenshot, attribution wording and any proof of purchase/permission in an asset register. Never remove watermarks or use preview files as production assets. Do not assume a clip is free for commercial use simply because it can be downloaded without payment.

VIDEO PRODUCTION AND WEB DELIVERY

Create web-ready derivatives from licensed originals. Choose formats and codecs supported by the target browser matrix, normally providing a broadly compatible MP4/H.264 version and considering WebM/AV1 only when the actual support and performance justify it. Use responsive resolutions and bitrates rather than serving a huge 4K file to every device. Provide a high-quality poster image for each clip. Use muted inline playback for background video, with playsinline and appropriate autoplay handling. Do not rely on autoplay with audio. Provide a pause or reduced-motion alternative where appropriate. Lazy-load clips that are not near the viewport and preload only the immediate next chapter when practical. Avoid downloading all chapter videos on initial page load.

The video must not block navigation or essential content. If a clip fails, is slow to load, or cannot autoplay, show its poster image and preserve the chapter text and actions. On mobile or constrained connections, use lower-resolution variants or still-image fallbacks. Respect data-saving preferences where detectable and avoid forcing large video downloads. Ensure the experience remains usable when the visitor scrolls quickly past a chapter. Manage playback and pause off-screen videos to reduce CPU, GPU and network use.

THREE.JS AND BLENDER SCOPE

Keep Three.js if it provides meaningful value for the globe, atmosphere, markers or transitions. Do not build a complicated 3D runtime solely to display rectangular video panels. If a simpler CSS/GSAP implementation achieves the same visual quality and reliability, prefer the simpler solution. Blender should be used for the globe and specific hero assets that need custom art direction, not as a requirement to model every destination. Low-poly, cartoonish, visibly generic or poorly textured assets are not acceptable for the main visual experience. Any third-party 3D asset must be checked for visual quality, technical suitability and commercial licence before use.

GSAP should coordinate the complete scroll narrative. A master timeline may control globe position/rotation, chapter copy, video opacity and playback states, overlays, destination markers and transitions. Avoid multiple competing timelines. Ensure scroll reversal restores the correct visual state and video behaviour. Since browser video playback is not inherently frame-perfect under scroll scrubbing, do not promise frame-accurate reverse playback unless a deliberate technique has been implemented and tested. Prefer reversible fades, crossfades and controlled playback/pause behaviour. If actual video scrubbing is used, test seeking performance, keyframe intervals, buffering and reverse-scroll behaviour across target browsers.

ACCESSIBILITY AND MOTION

All essential chapter text, navigation and calls to action must remain real HTML. Provide captions or transcripts when footage contains meaningful spoken content. Background footage should not contain essential information that is unavailable elsewhere. Respect prefers-reduced-motion by replacing large camera moves and autoplaying motion with still imagery or restrained transitions. Avoid flashing, rapid cuts and excessive motion. Ensure text contrast remains sufficient over every video frame, using carefully designed scrims or stable text panels when needed.

UPDATED PHASE GATES

Phase One must audit the existing project and produce a visual asset strategy comparing a modest 3D globe with real destination footage. It must identify the footage required for each chapter, candidate source libraries, licensing checks, approximate video formats/sizes, fallback strategy, and the proposed globe-to-footage transitions. It must not start large-scale modelling or download/use assets with unverified licences. Stop and wait for approval.

Phase Two must create a small proof of concept: one globe/intro moment and at least one representative real-footage chapter, with the transition, typography and scroll reversal demonstrated. Confirm the approach works on desktop and mobile before scaling to all chapters. Stop and wait for approval.

Phase Three must curate and prepare the remaining real-footage library and create only the 3D assets justified by the approved design. Maintain the asset and licence register. Phase Four integrates the full hybrid experience. The remaining platform, payment, security and QA phases remain as previously specified.

UPDATED ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

The visual result must look photorealistic and premium, not cartoon-like or like a generic template. Each regional chapter must use footage of real places with documented commercial-use rights. Footage must be visually coherent within its chapter, properly cropped, compressed and responsive. Video loading must not delay the initial experience unnecessarily. Poster fallbacks must work. Videos must pause when appropriate, and fast scrolling must not cause overlapping playback, excessive downloads or broken transitions. All transitions must reverse cleanly or use an explicitly documented non-scrub interaction that remains intuitive. Test desktop, mobile, reduced motion, slow network, failed video requests and browsers that restrict autoplay.

The final asset handoff must include a footage register with clip title, real location if known, creator, source page, licence, attribution, original filename, derivative filenames, duration, dimensions, codec, file size, chapter assignment and notes. Never invent the location represented by a clip. If the footage location is unknown or illustrative, do not label it as a specific real destination.
